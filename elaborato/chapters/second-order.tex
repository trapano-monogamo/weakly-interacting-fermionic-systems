\chapter{Ground State Energy in the Mean Field Limit}
\label{main-chapter}

To start our discussion of the ground state energy, we choose -- as stated in the introduction
-- a mean field scaling regime in the number of particles for both the kinetic and the
interaction terms in the Hamiltonian, for a system that has a smooth, two-body interaction
potential.

First, let us consider a system whose physical parameters, such as the mass of the fermions,
are such that the kinetic energy is simply given by $H_0 = \sum_{i=1}^N -\hbar^2\Delta_i$ and
the interaction by $\lambda \sum_{i<j} V(x_i-x_j)$. Second, because of Pauli's exclusion
principle, we know that -- at least for free fermions -- the ground state in momentum space
will be a ball centered around the origin whose radius depends on the number of particles, as
each momentum state can be occupied by at most one fermion (ignoring the degeneracy introduced
by other degrees of freedom, which are not relevant to our discussions). This is the Fermi
ball, and in three spatial dimensions the energy of a completely filled Fermi ball scales with
the number of particles as $N^{5/3}$ (the Fermi energy $\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}$ scales as
$N^{2/3}$, while the energy of the entire systems scales as $\epsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{2/5}$
\cite[p.~132]{ManBook}).
Introducing weak interactions between particles we get that the potential energy scales with
$N^2$, because the number of pair-wise interactions will be $N(N-1)/2 \sim N^2$ \cite{BenArt}.
Here, we are assuming that the potential itself $V$ is of order $\mathcal{O}(N^0)$\cite{HPRArt},
an assumption that can be realized by considering potentials such that the support of their
Fourier transform is compact and doesn't depend on $N$.

To retrieve a sensible, non-trivial limit for the energy as $N\to\infty$, we choose to rescale
the physical parameters \cite{BenArt} as
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:mean-field-scaling}
	\hbar = N^{-1/3},\quad\lambda = N^{-1}
\end{align}
where the redefinition of $\hbar$ is to be understood not as a redefinition of a fundamental
constant, but rather of a parameter of the system that ultimately represents the semi-classical
behavior of this scaling limit. Naming this parameter $\hbar$ is, at least in our setting,
just a matter of convenience.
With these prescriptions, the RPA ground state energy reproduces the scaling in the number of
particles $N$ that has been derived via exact methods in \cite{BenArt} and \cite{HPRArt}. In
particular, these results show the ground state energy is given by
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:ground-state-energy}
	E^{\mathrm{GS}}_N = E^{\mathrm{HF}}_N + E^{\mathrm{RPA}}_N
	+ \mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{3}-\alpha})
\end{align}
where
\begin{itemize}
	\item $E^{\mathrm{HF}}_N$ is the Hartree-Fock approximation for the ground state energy, containing
		the unperturbed energy of order $\mathcal{O}(N)$, a direct excitation contribution of
		order $\mathcal{O}(N)$ and a momentum exchange contribution of order $\mathcal{O}(N^0)$;
	\item $E^{\mathrm{RPA}}_N$ is the direct excitation contribution in the RPA and of order
		$\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/3})$;
	\item $\alpha$ is a positive real number that signals how the exchange correction to the RPA
		is subleading in $N$ with respect to the RPA itself.
\end{itemize}
In Theorem \ref{thm:main-result} -- the main result of this thesis -- we prove that the first
order in perturbation theory reproduces the expected scaling of Equation
(\ref{eq:ground-state-energy}) within the aforementioned scaling regime. We then comment on
the scaling of the non-problematic terms of the second order.

\section{Explicit calculation at second order}
\label{second-order}

The state space for a system of $N$ identical fermions in a box of side length $2\pi$ (for
convenience) with periodic boundary conditions is the space of completely antisymmetric
$L^2$-functions on the torus $\mathbb{T}^{3N} = (\mathbb{R}^3/(2\pi\mathbb{Z}^3))^N$:
\begin{align*}
	L^2_a(\mathbb{T}^{3N}) := \{\psi\in L^2(\mathbb{T}^{3N})
	: \psi(x_{\sigma(1)}, ..., x_{\sigma(N)})
	= \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\psi(x_1, ..., x_N) \,\forall \sigma \in S_N\}.
\end{align*}
The Hamiltonian for weakly interacting particles can be written as a sum of
the kinetic energy terms plus a sum of two-body interaction terms:
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:hamiltonian}
	H = \hbar^2 \sum_{i=1}^{N}(-\Delta_{x_i}) + \lambda \sum_{1 \leq i <
	j \leq N}V(x_i - x_j)
\end{align}
where we assume $V : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}$ to be a Schwartz function and its Fourier
transform to have compact support. This is motivated by the need to control the
convergence of the perturbative series, as we will discuss in detail in the rest of our work.
Of course, $V$ has period $2\pi$ in all variables, and we assume moreover that $V(x_i - x_j) =
V(|x_i - x_j|)$, which implies that $\hat{V}(-k) = \hat{V}(k)$.

Working in the grand canonical picture requires lifting this operator to the fermionic Fock
space, and requiring that the ground state energy is attained for a fixed number of particles
that is greater than zero means that the Hamiltonian picks up an additional term $-\mu\mathcal{N}$,
where $\mu$ is the \textit{chemical potential}. We absorb the chemical potential in the new
definition of the free Hamiltonian as $H_0=\sum_k(\hbar^2\vert k\vert^2-\mu)a^*_ka_k$. Putting
this all together, the Hamiltonian for this system is written as
\begin{align*}
	H = H_0 + \lambda V = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}^3} (\hbar^2|k|^2 - \mu) a^*_k a_k
	+ \sum_{k,p,q \in \mathbb{Z}^3} \lambda\hat{V}(k) a^*_{p-k} a^*_{q+k} a_q a_p
\end{align*}
where $a^\#_k=a^\#_-(\psi_k)$ are creation/annihilation operators on the fermionic Fock space
that create/annihilate states of definite momentum $k\in\mathbb{Z}^3$:
\begin{align*}
	\psi_k(x) = (2\pi)^{-\frac{3}{2}}e^{ik\cdot x}\quad\text{for}\,x\in\mathbb{T}^3.
\end{align*}

In the limit of zero temperature for a system of identical fermions, the chemical potential is
also a value between the highest energy level occupied and the first excited level. We can
therefore express it in terms of the number of particles in the ground state, as this
parameter serves as a constraint for such a system through the relation
\begin{align}
	\label{number-of-particles}
	N = \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} n_{\mathrm{F}}(\epsilon_k)
	\xlongrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{} \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \chi(\epsilon_k \leq \mu)
	= \vert B_{\mathrm{F}}\vert
\end{align}
where $k_{\mathrm{F}}$ is the Fermi momentum, defined by $\epsilon_{k_{\mathrm{F}}}=\mu$,
and $B_{\mathrm{F}}=\{p\in\mathbb{Z}^3:\vert p\vert\leq k_{\mathrm{F}}\}$ is the Fermi ball.
We re-derive the Fermi-Dirac distribution in Lemma \ref{two-point-function}. Equation
\eqref{number-of-particles} reduces to a counting problem for which we can use an argument by
Gauss, which gives a lower and an upper bound to the number of points on a lattice inside a
sphere. Namely, they are the number of unit cubes that are completely and at least partially
inside the sphere, respectively. The result of such bounds can be summed up into the estimate
given by the volume of the sphere plus an error proportional to the surface area:
\begin{align*}
	N = \vert B_{\mathrm{F}}\vert
	= \frac{4\pi}{3}k_{\mathrm{F}}^3 + \mathcal{O}(k_{\mathrm{F}}^2)
\end{align*}
which implies that
\begin{align}
	\label{fermi-surface}
	k_{\mathrm{F}} = \left(\frac{3}{4\pi}N\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} + \mathcal{O}(N^0).
\end{align}
Since we want to compute the second order contribution to the ground state energy, we will
calculate the free energy of the system and take its limit as the temperature goes to zero.
The free energy is given by
\begin{align*}
	F=-\frac{\log(Z)}{\beta}
\end{align*}
where $Z=\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})$ is the partition function, and we will compute this
logarithm perturbatively using Duhamel's formula, the subject of the following proposition.
Recall that at thermal equilibrium the expectation value of a bounded operator is given by the
Gibbs state functional, which is a quasi-free state, as stated in Theorem
\ref{mixed-quasi-free-wick}. We will take expectations with respect to $H_0$, so the Gibbs
state is given by
\begin{align*}
	\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\beta = \frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,e^{-\beta H_0})}{Z}.
\end{align*}

\begin{proposition}[Duhamel's formula]
	\label{prop:duhamel}
	Given $H = H_0 + \lambda V$ as defined above, the following approximation for the free
	energy holds formally at second order in $\lambda$, up to an overall factor of $-1/\beta$:
	\begin{align}
		\mathrm{log}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H}
		= \mathrm{log}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0}-\beta\lambda\langle V\rangle_\beta
		+\lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau \int_0^\tau\dx\tau' \left(
			\langle V(\tau')V(\tau)\rangle_\beta-\langle V(\tau')\rangle_\beta\langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
		\right).
	\end{align}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	Start by rewriting the operator $e^{-\beta H}e^{\beta H_0}$ using the
	fundamental theorem of calculus:
	\begin{align*}
		e^{-\beta H}e^{\beta H_0}
		&= \mathbbm{1}
			+ \int_0^\beta\dx\tau
			(\frac{\dx}{\dx t}e^{-t H}e^{t H_0})(\tau)\\
		&= \mathbbm{1}
			+ \int_0^\beta\dx\tau
			e^{-\tau H}(-H+H_0)e^{\tau H_0}\\
		&= \mathbbm{1}
			+ \int_0^\beta\dx\tau
			e^{-\tau H}e^{\tau H_0}e^{-\tau H_0}(-\lambda V)e^{\tau H_0}\\
		&= \mathbbm{1}
			- \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau
			e^{-\tau H}e^{\tau H_0}V(\tau) = \star
	\end{align*}
	where we defined $V(\tau)=e^{-\tau H_0}Ve^{\tau H_0}$. The integral is well
	defined as $\tau\mapsto\Vert e^{-\tau H}e^{\tau H_0}V(\tau)\Vert$ is a continuous function
	on the compact domain $[0,\beta]$ and is therefore Lebesgue integrable, which implies that
	$\tau\mapsto e^{-\tau H}e^{\tau H_0}V(\tau)$ is Bochner integrable. We briefly discuss
	integration of functions $\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{H})$ in Appendix
	\ref{appendix:bochner-integral}. Now we can see that in the integral we have the same term
	that appears in the l.h.s. but at a generic temperature $\tau$. Iterating this procedure
	just once gives us
	\begin{align*}
		\star &= \mathbbm{1}
				- \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau
				\left(
				\mathbbm{1} + \lambda\int_0^\tau\dx\tau'e^{-\tau' H}e^{\tau' H_0}V(\tau')
				\right)V(\tau) \\
			  &= \mathbbm{1} - \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau V(\tau)
				+ \lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau \int_0^\tau\dx\tau' V(\tau')V(\tau)
				+ \mathrm{R}(\lambda,V,\beta).
	\end{align*}
	where $\mathrm{R}(\lambda,V,\beta)$ is the remainder of this expression. Giving an
	estimate for the remainder that also allows to prove the convergence of this iterative
	process is a whole problem of its own, so we will simply truncate the expression and drop
	the remainder. Now we can invert this relation to isolate $e^{-\beta H}$ and take
	the trace. Since the trace is a continuous operator from trace class operators to $\mathbb{R}$
	and $Ve^{-\beta H_0}$ is trace class, we can move it inside the integral (see Appendix
	\ref{appendix:special-operators} and Appendix \ref{appendix:bochner-integral}) to get:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{Tr} \, e^{-\beta H}
		&=
			\mathrm{Tr} \, e^{-\beta H_0}
			- \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\, \mathrm{Tr}(V(\tau)e^{-\beta H_0})
			+ \lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\int_0^\tau\dx\tau'\, \mathrm{Tr}(V(\tau')V(\tau)e^{-\beta H_0})\\
		&= (\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0})\left(
			1 - \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\,\langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
			+ \lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\int_0^\tau\dx\tau'\, \langle (V(\tau')V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
			\right).
	\end{align*}
	We can now take the logarithm on both sides. Recall the Taylor expansion
	$\mathrm{log}(1+x) \sim x - x^2/2\,$ as $x \to 0$ and apply it to the r.h.s.,
	but note that the first integral gives two contributions (one at first
	order and one at second order) while the second integral only contributes to the second order.
	In the end, we are left with:
	\begin{align}
		\label{eq:duhamel}
		\mathrm{log}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H}
		&= \mathrm{log}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0}
			- \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\,\langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
			- \frac{1}{2} \left( \lambda\int_0^\beta\dx\tau \langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta \right)^2 \nonumber \\
		&\quad + \lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\int_0^\tau\dx\tau'\, \langle (V(\tau')V(\tau) \rangle_\beta \nonumber \\
		&= \mathrm{log}\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0}
			- \beta \lambda\langle V \rangle_\beta \nonumber \\
		&\quad+ \lambda^2\int_0^\beta\dx\tau\int_0^\tau\dx\tau'\,\biggl(
				\langle V(\tau')V(\tau) \rangle_\beta - \langle V(\tau') \rangle_\beta \langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
			\biggr)
	\end{align}
	where in the last step we performed the first integral and rewrote the square as a double integral,
	then taking into account the symmetry under $\tau \longleftrightarrow \tau'$
	we could remove the factor $1/2$ by integrating only over the lower triangle $\{(\tau,\tau')\in[0,\beta]^2 : \tau' \leq \tau\}$.
\end{proof}

It is possible to see that iterating Duhamel's formula leads exactly to the definition of the
$\tau$-ordered exponential $\mathcal{T}[e^{-\beta H}]$, and at every order the integrand looks
exactly like the content of Theorem \ref{thm:linked-cluster}. To prove that the perturbative
series in the operator formalism and in Grassmann variables are formally identical, we need to
identify operator expectations $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\beta$ with Grassmann expectations
$\mathbb{E}_G(\,\cdot\,)$, but also identify the propagator $\langle a^*_k(\tau)a_{k'}(\tau')
\rangle_\beta$ with the covariance $G_{k,k'}(\tau,\tau')$.
\\
To introduce Grassmann variables we require the system to have a finite number of physical
modes. Momentum modes can be made finite by introducing a cutoff $\Lambda$:
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{D}_\Lambda := \{ k\in\mathbb{Z}^3 : \vert k\vert < \Lambda \} = \mathbb{Z}^3\cap\mathbb{B}_\Lambda(0),
	\quad\mathrm{with}\;\vert\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\vert = M,
\end{align*}
but the propagator depends on the temperature of the operators, which is a continuous
parameter. To fix this issue we should resort to using \textit{Matsubara frequencies} and
introduce their own cutoff $\Omega$. We point to Appendix \ref{matsubara} for a brief
discussion on this topic.
\\
Usually this procedure doesn't cause any issues, and in the following calculations we do not
concern ourselves with it, instead we will assume that we can remove the cutoff $\Omega$ and
work directly with continuous temperature.

Let then $\boldsymbol{\psi} = (\psi_1, ..., \psi_M)$ and
$\boldsymbol{\bar\psi} = (\bar\psi_1, ..., \bar\psi_M)$ be the Grassmann variables
associated to the families of operators $a$ and $a^*$ respectively.
Having introduced the cutoff, the kinetic part of the Hamiltonian can now be written as a
simple finite linear combination of fermionic number operators, which makes it a
bounded operator:
\begin{align*}
	H_0 = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{D}_\Lambda} (\epsilon_k-\mu) a^*_k a_k
\end{align*}
The full physical theory will then be recovered in the limit $\Lambda\to\infty$ (and $\Omega
\to\infty)$.

We begin the calculation by building the Gaussian measure for the expectations $\langle\,\cdot
\,\rangle_\beta$. Note that expectations of the form $\langle V(\tau)V(\tau')\rangle_\beta$
contain operators at different temperatures.

\begin{lemma}
	\label{two-point-function}
	The two-point function for this system has the following form:
	\begin{equation}
		G_{k,k'}(\tau,\tau')
		= \langle a^*_k(\tau) a_{k'}(\tau') \rangle_\beta
		= \delta_{k,k'} n_{\mathrm{F}}(\epsilon_k) e^{(\tau'-\tau)(\epsilon_{k}-\mu)}
	\end{equation}
	where $n_\mathrm{F}(\epsilon) = (e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1)^{-1}$ is the Fermi-Dirac distribution
	at temperature $\beta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let us first focus on finding an expression for field operators at a given temperature
	$a^\#(\tau)$. Recall from the proof of Theorem \ref{prop:duhamel} that for an operator $A$
	its evolution at temperature $\tau$ is given by $A(\tau)=e^{-\tau H_0}A e^{\tau H_0}$. Once
	again we will make use of the fundamental theorem of calculus to write
	\begin{align*}
		e^{-\tau H_0}a_k e^{\tau H_0}
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau \dx\lambda\, \frac{\dx}{\dx\lambda}\left( e^{-\lambda H_0}a_k e^{\lambda H_0} \right) \\
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau \dx\lambda\, e^{-\lambda H_0}(-H_0 a_k + a_k H_0)e^{\lambda H_0} \\
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau \dx\lambda\, e^{-\lambda H_0}[a_k,H_0]e^{\lambda H_0} \\
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau \dx\lambda\, e^{-\lambda H_0}\sum_{k'\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}(\epsilon_{k'}-\mu)[a_k,a^*_{k'} a_{k'}]e^{\lambda H_0}
		= \star.
	\end{align*}
	Recall that, for operators $A$, $B$ and $C$ defined on a common domain, it holds that
	\begin{align*}
		[A,BC] = \{A,B\}C - B\{A,C\}
	\end{align*}
	as can be shown by explicitly writing the expressions for the anticommutators. Using this
	identity we rewrite the commutator that appears in the last expression in terms of the
	canonical anticommutator as follows:
	\begin{align*}
		\star
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau\dx\lambda\, e^{-\lambda H_0}
			\sum_{k'\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}(\epsilon_{k'}-\mu)(\{a_k,a^*_{k'}\}a_{k'} - a^*_{k'}\{a_k,a_{k'}\})
			e^{\lambda H_0} \\
		&= a_k + \int_0^\tau\dx\lambda\, e^{-\lambda H_0}(\epsilon_{k}-\mu)a_{k}e^{\lambda H_0} \\
		&= a_k + (\epsilon_k-\mu)\int_0^\tau\dx\lambda\,a_k(\lambda)
	\end{align*}
	where we used that $\{a_k,a^*_{k'}\}=\delta_{k,k'}$ and that $\{a_k,a_{k'}\}=0$. We now
	have an expression for $a_k(\tau)$ in terms of its integral, and if we take the derivative
	with respect to $\tau$ we get the following Cauchy problem:
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{cases}
			\frac{\dx}{\dx\tau}a_k(\tau) = (\epsilon_k-\mu) a_k(\tau) \\
			a_k(0) = a_k
		\end{cases}.
	\end{align*}
	Using Theorem \ref{thm:ODE}, it suffices to find one such solution explicitly, which is:
	\begin{align*}
		a_k(\tau) = e^{\tau(\epsilon_k-\mu)}a_k(0) = e^{\tau(\epsilon_k-\mu)}a_k.
	\end{align*}
	The procedure is completely analogous in the case of $a^*_k(\tau)$ and the result differs
	only by a sign in the exponent:
	\begin{align*}
		a^*_k(\tau) = e^{-\tau(\epsilon_k-\mu)}a^*_k(0) = e^{-\tau(\epsilon_k-\mu)}a^*_k.
	\end{align*}
	By linearity, we can take the scalar exponential factors out of the expectation value to
	write the two-point function in terms of the quantity $\langle a^*_k a_{k'}\rangle_\beta$:
	\begin{align*}
		\langle a^*_k(\tau) a_{k'}(\tau') \rangle_\beta
		= e^{-\tau(\epsilon_k-\mu)}e^{\tau'(\epsilon_{k'}-\mu)} \langle a^*_k a_{k'} \rangle_\beta.
	\end{align*}

	It remains to determine the expectation $\langle a^*_k a_{k'}\rangle_\beta$. Recall that
	in the fermionic case the occupation number basis decomposes the Fock space like we
	discussed in Proposition \ref{occupation-number-basis}. Using the cutoff on high momentum,
	the trace in the Gibbs state functional becomes a sum over a finite amount of states. Let
	$\{\vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle = \vert n_1\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes\vert n_M\rangle
	: n_i\in\{0,1\}\;\forall i=1,\ldots,M\}$
	be the complete orthonormal set of eigenvectors of $M$ independent number density operators
	(in our case, $M=\vert\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\vert$). Using the momenta
	$k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda$ as indices for the basis states labels $n_k$, we write
	\begin{align*}
		\langle a^*_k a_{k'} \rangle_\beta
		&= \frac{\mathrm{Tr}(a^*_k a_{k'}e^{-\beta H_0})}{\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0}}
		= \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\{0,1\}^M}\langle\boldsymbol{n}\vert e^{-\beta H_0}a^*_k a_{k'}\vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\{0,1\}^M}\langle\boldsymbol{n}\vert a^*_k a_k \vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\prod_{p\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)n_p}\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{\boldsymbol{n}\in\{0,1\}^M} n_k e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)n_k} \prod_{p\neq k} e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)n_p}\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{1}{Z} \left(\sum_{n_k=0}^1 n_k e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)n_k}\right)\prod_{p\neq k}\sum_{n_p=0}^1 e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)n_p}\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{1}{Z} e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}\prod_{p\neq k}(1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)})
	\end{align*}
	where we used the fact that $H_0$ is bounded and diagonal in the occupation number basis
	$\{\vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle\}$, and that $a^*_k a_k\vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle=n_k\vert\boldsymbol{n}\rangle$.
	Using many of the same steps for the denominator, we find that
	$Z=\prod_{p\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)})$, and thus the expression
	simplifies to
	\begin{align*}
		\langle a^*_k a_{k'}\rangle_\beta
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}\prod_{p\neq k}(1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)})}{\prod_{p}(1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)})}\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}}{1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}}\\
		&= \delta_{k,k'} \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}+1}.
	\end{align*}
	This is exactly the Fermi-Dirac distribution, and the thesis follows immediately.
\end{proof}

With the 2-point function determined we can go over to the computation via Feynman diagrams.
Recall the definition for the expectation of a function of Grassmann variables
\begin{align*}
	\mathbb{E}_G(\,\cdot\,) = \int P_G(\dx\psi)(\,\cdot\,)\, , \quad \text{where}\;
	P_G(\dx\psi) = \frac{1}{\mathrm{det}\,G} \left( \prod_{j=1}^N \dx\psi_j\dx\bar\psi_j \right)
	e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi}\cdot G^{-1}\boldsymbol{\psi}}
\end{align*}
and the graphical representation of the interaction term via a vertex, which in our case is
exactly the quartic term we discussed in Section \ref{sec:toy-model}:
\begin{align*}
	V = \sum_{k,p,q} \hat{V}(k) \bar\psi_{p-k} \bar\psi_{q+k} \psi_q \psi_p = \;
	\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8, baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
		\node (a) at (-1, 1) {} node[scale=0.7] at (-1.1, 0.5) {$p-k$};
		\node (b) at ( 1, 1) {} node[scale=0.7] at ( 1.1, 0.5) {$q+k$};
		\node (c) at (-1,-1) {} node[scale=0.7] at (-0.9,-0.5) {$q$};
		\node (d) at ( 1,-1) {} node[scale=0.7] at ( 0.9,-0.5) {$p$};
		\begin{scope}[very thick, decoration={
				markings,
				mark=at position 0.57 with {\arrow{>}}}
			]
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (0,0) -- (a);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (0,0) -- (b);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (c) -- (0,0);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (d) -- (0,0);
		\end{scope}
		\filldraw[black] (0,0) circle (2pt);
	\end{tikzpicture}.
\end{align*}

We can evaluate the expectations that show up in Duhamel's formula separately by following the
diagrammatic rules we discussed in the previous chapter. Let's start with $\langle V\rangle_\beta$.
Omitting the temperatures when writing two-point functions (as they are evaluated at the same
temperatures) and calling $n_k=n_\text{F}(\epsilon_k)$ the calculation is as follows:
\begin{align*}
	\langle V \rangle_\beta
	&= \mathbb{E}_G \biggl(
		\sum_{k,p,q} \hat{V}(k) \bar\psi_{p-k} \bar\psi_{q+k} \psi_q \psi_p
		\biggr)
	= \sum_{\Gamma \in \mathcal{G}_{\text{C},1}} \text{val}(\Gamma) \\
	&= \vcenter{\hbox{
		\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.5, baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
			\node[shape=coordinate] (a) at (-1, 1) {};
			\node[shape=coordinate] (b) at ( 1.1, 1.1) {};

Let us now consider the term $\langle V(\tau')V(\tau)\rangle_\beta-\langle V(\tau')

the problems with the second order arise. This time we have terms with
two vertices, and using Theorem \ref{thm:linked-cluster} the expression factorizes as follows,
recalling the identification of Gibbs expectations $\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle_\beta$ with
Grassmann expectations $\mathbb{E}_G(\,\cdot\,)$:
\begin{align*}
	\langle V(\tau')V(\tau) \rangle_\beta - \langle V(\tau') \rangle_\beta\langle V(\tau) \rangle_\beta
	&= \mathbb{E}_G(V(\tau')V(\tau)) - \mathbb{E}_G(V(\tau'))\mathbb{E}_G(V(\tau)) \\
	&= \mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{T}}_G(V(\tau');V(\tau))\\
	&= \sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{\mathrm{C},2}} \mathrm{val}(\Gamma).
\end{align*}
There are exactly two distinct topologies for the second order connected diagrams: those with
no tadpoles, and those with two tadpoles. To see this, let us call A and B the two vertices
of a connected diagram, and analyze the two topologies separately.
\begin{itemize}
	\item{\textbf{No tadpoles.}} Consider one propagator exiting A (the other exiting A will be
		fixed by this choice): it has two possible destinations in B, both of which leave only
		two possible ways to complete the diagram. If, at first, we choose the other leg
		exiting A we would produce exact duplicates of the diagrams we obtained so far. We
		conclude that this topology admits 4 diagrams.
	\item{\textbf{Two tadpoles.}} There are 4 ways to put a tadpole on a vertex, up to a total
		of 16 possible ways of having a diagram with two tadpoles. Each of this configurations
		has to a unique way to complete the diagram, thus we conclude that this topology
		admits 16 diagrams.
\end{itemize}

	\exists\,p,q\in\mathbb{Z}^3 \;\textrm{such that}\; \epsilon_{p-k}=\epsilon_p \;\textrm{and}\; \epsilon_{q+k}=\epsilon_q \right\}.
\end{align*}

We proceed with the analysis of the zeroth and first orders in this expression, and postpone
the comments on the peculiarities of the second order to the next, and last, section.
\\
The next step is to show that removing the UV cutoff, i.e., taking the limit
$\Lambda\to\infty$, leads to a well-defined expression. To prove this we do not need to use
the specific expression $\epsilon_k=\hbar^2|k|^2$ for the kinetic energy, instead we only
assume a condition on its asymptotic behavior for high momentum. A slight generalization
which costs us nothing. In fact, we may also assume a weaker condition on the potential and
drop the condition that $\hat{V}$ has compact support, as we can control its growth quite
effectively -- at least until we get to the zero temperature limit, when we will restore the
initial condition.
\\
A quick comment on a slightly less standard notation we will use. We say that two real or
complex sequences $\{x_n\}_n$ and $\{y_n\}_n$ are asymptotic if, and only if, $x_n/y_n
\longrightarrow 1$. We will indicate that two quantities are asymptotic only up to a
proportionality constant by adopting the following notation:
\begin{align*}
	x_n \asymp y_n \iff \exists\, C\neq0 : \frac{x_n}{y_n} \longrightarrow C.
\end{align*}
\\
To control terms that contain the Fourier transform of the interaction potential we will make
use of the following property:
\begin{lemma}
	\label{fourier-decay}
	Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ be a Schwartz function, then for all $m\in\mathbb{N}$
	it holds that
	\begin{align*}
		\vert \hat{f}(k)\vert \leq \frac{1}{\vert k\vert^m}
			\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\dx x\,\vert \partial^\alpha f(x)\vert.
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Recall the expression for $\hat{f}(k)$ and note that we may rewrite the exponential in it
	as a derivative, then integrate by parts (the boundary term vanishes as $f$ is a Schwartz
	function):
	\begin{align*}
		\hat{f}(k)
		= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\dx^nx\, e^{ik\cdot x}f(x)
		= \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\dx^nx\, \left(\frac{1}{ik_j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}e^{ik\cdot x}\right)f(x)
		= \frac{-1}{ik_j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\dx^nx\,e^{ik\cdot x} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}f(x).
	\end{align*}
	We iterate this procedure $m$ times to get get a derivative of $f$ of order $m$. Note that
	every time we integrate by parts the boundary term vanishes because all derivatives must
	vanish at infinity. Lastly we bound $\vert\hat{f}(k)\vert$ by taking the modulus inside
	the integral:
	\begin{align*}
		\vert\hat{f}(k)\vert \leq
		\frac{1}{\vert k\vert^m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\dx^nx\, \left\vert\frac{\partial^m}{\partial x_{j_1}\cdots\partial x_{j_m}}f(x)\right\vert,
	\end{align*}
	where $j_1,\ldots,j_m$ are used to indicate any derivative of order $m$, and we also used
	that $\vert k_j\vert\leq\sqrt{(k_1)^2+\cdots+(k_n)^2}=\vert k\vert$. This concludes the
	proof.
\end{proof}

We now prove that we can remove the UV cutoff.

\begin{theorem}
	\label{removing-cutoff}
	Asuume that $\hat{V}$ is a Schwartz function, and assume moreover that $\epsilon_k$
	depends only on $\vert k\vert$ and that it satisfies
	$\lim_{\vert k\vert\to\infty}\epsilon_k/\vert k\vert>0$. Then the first order in Equation
	(\ref{final-expression}) converges in the limit $\Lambda\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	The zeroth order is trivial, as it doesn't depend on $\Lambda$ and is, in fact, finite:
	$e^{-\beta H_0}$ is trace-class and strictly positive definite, which implies
	$\vert\log\,\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta H_0}\vert<\infty$. Before moving on let's discuss the
	estimates we will use. As a consequence of Lemma \ref{fourier-decay} we have that $\vert
	\hat{V}(k)\vert\leq C_{1,m}/\vert k\vert^m$ for some $C_{1,m}>0$ that does not depend on
	$\Lambda$, and for all $m\in\mathbb{N}$. As for the energy terms, we are assuming that
	$\epsilon_k\geq C_{2,m}\vert k\vert$ for all $k$ outside of the ball of radius
	$K$ for some $K,C_{2,m}>0$ that again do not depend on $\Lambda$. Finally, the Fermi-Dirac
	distribution is controlled by an exponential decay in $\epsilon_k$, which is bounded at
	least by an exponential decay in $\vert k\vert$.
	\\
	Looking at the first order, we can write
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{p,k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda} \left\vert\left(\hat{V}(0)-\hat{V}(k)\right) n_p n_{p-k}\right\vert
		&\leq \vert\hat{V}(0)\vert \sum_{p,k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}n_p n_{p-k}
		+ \sum_{p,k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}\vert\hat{V}(k)\vert n_p n_{p-k}\\
		&= \vert\hat{V}(0)\vert \sum_{p\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}n_p\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}n_{p-k}
		+ \sum_{p\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}n_p\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda}\vert\hat{V}(k)\vert n_{p-k}.
	\end{align*}
	By the positivity of the general terms for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}^3$ we can bound the sums by
	the respective series over $\mathbb{Z}^3$, and because the general terms only depend on
	the modulus of the momentum we can employ the following strategy. Define the spherical
	shell $A_n = \{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3 : n \leq \vert k\vert < n+1\}$ and write a series with
	general term $b_{\vert k\vert}$ as
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} b_{\vert k\vert} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \sum_{k\in A_n} b_{\vert k\vert}.
	\end{align*}
	Because $c_1\vert A_n\vert b_{n+1} \leq \sum_{k\in A_n}b_{\vert k\vert} \leq c_2
	\vert A_{n+1}\vert b_n$ for all $n$ if $a_n$ is decreasing, and because $\vert A_n\vert
	\asymp n^2$, we can conclude that the behavior of $\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3}
	b_{\vert k\vert}$ and $\sum_{n=0}^\infty n^2 b_n$ is the same. Note that
	$\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3}n_k$ converges if, and only if, the series $\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
	\ell^2 e^{-\beta \epsilon_\ell}$ converges, by the asymptotic comparison test. The latter
	does converge absolutely as shown by the root test:
	\begin{align*}
		\left\vert \ell^2 e^{-\beta\epsilon_{\ell}}\right\vert^{1/\ell}
		= e^{\frac{2\log(\ell) - \beta\epsilon_\ell}{\ell}}
		\xlongrightarrow[\ell\to\infty]{} Q \in [0,1).
	\end{align*}
	Similarly, $\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3}\vert\hat{V}(k)\vert n_k$ converges too. We split the
	series into a finite sum over a ball $D$ centered at the origin and of finite radius plus
	the sum over the complement, and the latter converges, as
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{k\in D^\mathrm{C}} \vert\hat{V}(k)\vert n_k
		\leq \sum_{k\in D^\mathrm{C}}\frac{C_1 n_k}{\vert k\vert^m}
		\leq \sum_{k\in D^\mathrm{C}} C_1 n_k.
	\end{align*}
	Thus, in the limit in $\Lambda\to\infty$, the first order is an absolutely convergent series.
\end{proof}

This second order should, in principle, contain direct and exchange contributions, as we
discussed at the beginning of this chapter, but let us simply truncate to first order this
expression, at least for now. Dividing by $-\beta$ to get the free energy leads to:
\begin{align}
	\label{free-energy}
	F = F^{(0)} + F_{\mathrm{D}}^{(1)} + F_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)}
	= F^{(0)}
	+\lambda\sum_{p,k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \hat{V}(0) n_p n_{p-k}
	-\lambda\sum_{p,k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \hat{V}(k) n_p n_{p-k}
\end{align}
where each term has been named appropriately following the order in which they appear: $F^{(0)}$
is the free energy of the unperturbed system (no interactions), $F_{\mathrm{D}}^{(1)}$ is the
direct term at first order and $F_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)}$ is the exchange term at second order.

\section{Mean field scaling}

With the convergence of the free energy under control, the next step is to look at the limit
$\beta\to\infty$ to retrieve the ground state energy and complete our proof of the first order
of Equation (\ref{eq:ground-state-energy}).

\begin{lemma}
	\label{zero-temperature}
	With the same hypotheses as in Theorem \ref{removing-cutoff}, each term in Equation
	(\ref{free-energy}) converges uniformly in $\beta$ over $[\delta,\infty)$ for some
	$\delta>0$, and the limit $\beta \to \infty$ can thus be taken inside the sums.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	The only troubling factor is the Fermi-Dirac distribution, as it has a discontinuity
	at $\epsilon_k = \mu$ in the limit $\beta\to\infty$. By splitting each sum into a finite
	part up to, say, $\epsilon_k = 2\mu$, and a series over the remaining values of $k$, we
	can avoid this discontinuity. Moreover, we are restricting the domain on which to check
	for uniform convergence to $[\delta,\infty)$ for some $\delta>0$ because we are considering
	a limit away from $\beta=0$. With these things in mind we can see that the series
	$\sum_k n_k$ converges uniformly, as
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{k:\epsilon_k\geq2\mu} n_k
		\leq \sum_k \sup_{\beta\in[\delta,\infty)} n_k
		\leq \sum_k e^{-\delta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}
	\end{align*}
	and the upper bound converges absolutely by the same arguments used in the previous
	theorem. Modifying this series by multiplying the general term by $\hat{V}(k)$ does not
	change the result, as it does not depend on $\beta$ and still lead to an absolutely
	convergent series as an upper bound. We can see that, by rewriting sums of the form
	$\sum_{p,q}(...)$ as $\sum_q(...\sum_p(...))$, we only need to check the
	convergence of the innermost series in all the terms of Equation (\ref{free-energy}), and
	the rest will follow from arguments that we already covered. Consider the term
	$F^{(1)}_{\mathrm{D}}+F^{(1)}_{\mathrm{X}}$:
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{p,k} \left|\hat{V}(0)-\hat{V}(k)\right| n_p n_{p-k}
		&= \sum_p n_p \sum_k \left|\hat{V}(0)-\hat{V}(k)\right| n_{p-k}\\
		&\leq \sum_p n_p \sum_k \left|\hat{V}(0)-\hat{V}(k)\right| \sup_{\beta\in[\delta,\infty)}n_{p-k}
		= \star
	\end{align*}
	but the series containing $\sup_\beta(...)$ converges to some $C>0$ as we discussed above,
	and we can therefore write $\star = \sum_p C n_p$, which again converges. With this,
	the first term converges uniformly in $\beta$ over $[\delta,\infty)$.
\end{proof}

Now that we have proved that we can take the limit of zero temperature inside the series, we
are left with the task of computing it. The zero temperature Fermi-Dirac distribution is just
the characteristic function of the Fermi ball $B_{\mathrm{F}}$:
\begin{align*}
	n_{\mathrm{F}}(\epsilon) \xrightarrow[\beta\to\infty]{} \chi(\epsilon\leq\mu).
\end{align*}
This turns sums in Equation \eqref{free-energy} into finite sums over intersecting Fermi
balls. We present a useful lemma that will help us control the growth of these sums as $N$
grows.

\begin{lemma}[Riemann sum approximation]
	\label{riemann}
	Let $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$ be a compactly supported, Lipschitz continuous function,
	with Lipschitz constant L. Then for $N\in\mathbb{N}$ we have the following approximation
	by integration, with $m\in\mathbb{N}$ and $h=N^{-1/m}$ the lattice spacing:
	\begin{align*}
		N^{-\frac{d}{m}}\sum_{k\in h\mathbb{Z}^d} f(k)
		= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\dx^dx\,f(x) + \mathcal{E}(h),
	\end{align*}
	where $\mathcal{E}(h)\leq hLK_f\sqrt{d} = \mathcal{O}(h)$, and $K_f$ a constant that
	depends on the volume of the support of $f$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let $h=N^{-1/m}$ be the lattice spacing in $N^{-1/m}\mathbb{Z}^d$. Then define, at each
	point in the lattice, a cell of side length $h$ as
	\begin{align*}
		C_k \coloneq [hk_1,h(k_1+1)]\times\cdots\times[hk_d,h(k_d+1)].
	\end{align*}
	Note that the volume of a cell is $\int_{C_k}\dx^dx=h^d=N^{-d/m}$. We can estimate the error of
	integration through the difference between the sum and the integral. The domain
	of integration is split into cells that intersect on sets of measure zero, and the
	integrand has compact support, so the integral can be split as a sum of integrals over the
	individual cells. Changing variables with $k\mapsto k'=k/h$ we can write
	\begin{align*}
		\left\vert N^{-\frac{d}{m}}\sum_{k\in h\mathbb{Z}^d}f(k) - \int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\dx^dx\,f(x) \right\vert
		&= \left\vert N^{-\frac{d}{m}}\sum_{k\in \mathbb{Z}^d}f(hk) - \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\int_{C_k}\dx^dx\,f(x) \right\vert\\
		&= \left\vert \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\int_{C_k}\dx^dx\,\chi_{\mathrm{supp}(f)}(x)(f(hk)-f(x))\right\vert\\
		&\leq L\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\chi_{\mathrm{supp}(f)}(hk)\int\limits_{C_k\,\cap\,\mathrm{supp}(f)}\dx^dx\,\vert hk-x\vert
		= \star,
	\end{align*}
	where in the last step we used Lipschitz continuity. Now we can bound the integral of
	$\vert hk-x\vert$ on $C_k$ by $h\sqrt{d}=\max_{C_k}(\vert hk-x\vert)$ times the
	volume of a cell $C_k$, to get that
	\begin{align*}
		\star \leq L\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\chi_{\mathrm{supp}(f)}(hk)h\sqrt{d}\left(\int_{C_{k}}\dx^dx\right)
		= hL\sqrt{d}N^{-\frac{d}{m}}\mathcal{O}(N^{\frac{d}{m}}) = \mathcal{O}(h).
	\end{align*}
	where we used that $\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^d}\chi_{\mathrm{supp}(f)}(hk)$ is of order $N^{d/m}$
	because $f$ is compactly supported. The thesis follows immediately from this bound, as
	$h = N^{-1/m}$.
\end{proof}

For each term in Equation \eqref{free-energy}, we write $E$ instead of $F$ to indicate the
same term in the limit $\beta\to\infty$. That is, $E_\mathrm{gs} = \lim_{\beta\to\infty}F =
E^{(0)} + E^{(1)}_\mathrm{D} + E^{(1)}_\mathrm{X}$.

\begin{theorem}
	\label{thm:main-result}
	The ground state energy of the Hamiltonian in (\ref{eq:hamiltonian}) exhibits the
	following scalings in the number of particles $N$ at first order in perturbation theory:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $E^{(0)} = \mathcal{O}(N)$,
		\item $E_{\mathrm{D}}^{(1)} = \mathcal{O}(N)$ and $E_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)} = \mathcal{O}(N^0)$,
	\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Recall that $B_{\mathrm{F}}=\{p\in\mathbb{Z}^3:\vert p\vert<k_{\mathrm{F}}\}$, we can
	expand the expression for $F^{(0)}$ as
	\begin{align*}
		F_0
		&= -\frac{\log(Z)}{\beta} = -\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \frac{1}{\beta}\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)})
		= \sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}(\epsilon_p-\mu) + \sum_{p\not\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}\log(1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_p-\mu)}),
	\end{align*}
	where in the limit the second term vanishes. From here we can change the index summed over
	in $\sum_{\vert p\vert\leq CN^{1/3}}$ to approximate the result with an integral. Recall
	the mean field regime defined in Equation \eqref{eq:mean-field-scaling} and that
	$k_{\mathrm{F}}= \mathcal{O}(N^{1/3})$. We take $p\mapsto p'=N^{-1/3}p$, which means that
	in the Fermi ball it holds $\vert p'\vert\leq C$. Using Lemma \ref{riemann} we come to the
	approximation
	\begin{align*}
		\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}(\hbar^2p^2-\mu)
		&= N^{-\frac{2}{3}}\sum_{\substack{p\in\mathbb{Z}^3:\\\vert k\vert\leq CN^{1/3}}}p^2 -\mu\sum_{p\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}1\\
		&= N^{-\frac{2}{3}}N\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\substack{p'\in N^{-1/3}\mathbb{Z}^3:\\\vert p'\vert\leq C}} (N^{\frac{1}{3}}p')^2\right)-N\mu\\
		&= N \left(\int_0^C 4\pi\dx p\,p^4 + \mathcal{O}(N^{-\frac{1}{3}})\right) -N\mu\\
		&= \mathcal{O}(N)
	\end{align*}
	where we used that, from Equation (\ref{fermi-surface}), $\mu=\hbar^2k_{\mathrm{F}}^2=
	\mathcal{O}(1)$. This shows that indeed $E^{(0)}$ is of order $N$. Onto the first order,
	recall the scaling limit $\lambda=N^{-1}$ to see that
	\begin{align*}
		E_{\mathrm{D}}^{(1)}+E_{\mathrm{X}}^{(1)}
		&= \lambda\sum_{\substack{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3,p\in B_{\mathrm{F}}:\\p-k\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}}\left(\hat{V}(0)-\hat{V}(k)\right)
		= N^{-1}\hat{V}(0)\left(\sum_{p,k\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}1\right)
		- N^{-1}\sum_{\substack{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3,p\in B_{\mathrm{F}}:\\p-k\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}}\hat{V}(k)\\
		&= \mathcal{O}(N) + N^{-1}\mathcal{O}(N) = \mathcal{O}(N) + \mathcal{O}(N^0).
	\end{align*}
	where in the last steps we used the condition that $\mathrm{supp}(\hat{V})$ is compact, so
	that $\sum_k\hat{V}(k)=\mathcal{O}(1)$ and $\sum_{p,k}\hat{V}(k)=\mathcal{O}(N)$. With
	this expression we can conclude that the first order indeed contributes to order
	$\mathcal{O}(N)$ with the direct term and to order $\mathcal{O}(1)$ with the exchange term.
\end{proof}

\section{On this first attempt at second order perturbation theory}

If we look at the second order we can immediately see what difficulties arise in applying the
proofs we covered so far to its expression.
We briefly discuss the UV cutoff and the zero temperature limit, and then point out the core
question that we will leave unanswered in this work: the $N$-scaling of the second order.
Let us call the two well defined contributions the direct $F^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}}$ and the
exchange $F^{(2)}_{\mathrm{X}}$ contributions:
\begin{align}
	\label{bad-second-order}
	F^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}} + F^{(2)}_{\mathrm{X}}
	&= 2\lambda^2\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\\k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{M}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)^2 n_p n_q n_{q+k} n_{p-k}}{\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p}
	\biggl(1 + \frac{1-e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p)}}{\beta(\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p)} \biggr)\nonumber\\
	&\quad+ 2\lambda^2\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\\k\in\mathcal{D}_\Lambda\setminus\mathcal{M}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)\hat{V}(p-q-k) n_p n_q n_{q+k} n_{p-k}}{\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p}
	\biggl(1 + \frac{1-e^{-\beta(\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p)}}{\beta(\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p)} \biggr).
\end{align}

We first discuss the removal of the UV cutoff. The proof for the second order can only be
partial, as the expression is not well defined for all momenta in the series, in particular,
we are explicitly removing the set $\mathcal{M}$ from the allowed momenta. For direct term we
can observe that, for fixed $p$ and $q$, the energy terms that depend on $k$ dominate the
denominator for $k$ far enough from the origin:
\begin{align*}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)^2n_pn_qn_{p-k}n_{q+k}}{\vert \epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p\vert}
	\sim \frac{\hat{V}(k)^2n_pn_qn_{p-k}n_{q+k}}{\vert \epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}\vert}
\end{align*}
and the factor $(\beta+(\ldots))$ doesn't change the asymptotics of this expression.
Turning sums over $\mathbb{Z}^3$ into series over spherical shells while avoiding the
problematic momeanta leads to an absolutely convergent series by the same arguments used in
the proof of Theorem \ref{removing-cutoff}. The same argument can be applied to the exchange
term, again avoiding momenta for which the denominators are zero. We will still replace the
sums over $\mathcal{D}_\Lambda$ in Equation \eqref{bad-second-order} with series over
$\mathbb{Z}^3$ and move onto the zero temperature limit.
\\
At first order, we carried out the zero temperature limit by proving that the series were
uniformly convergent in $\beta$. We now discuss how a similar procedure may apply at second
order. Focusing on the innermost series in $k$ in the direct term, we have the two following
series to discuss:
\begin{align*}
	A&\coloneq\sum_k \frac{\hat{V}(k)^2n_pn_qn_{q+k}n_{p-k}}{\epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_p-\epsilon_q}
	\\
	B&\coloneq\sum_k \frac{\hat{V}(k)^2n_pn_qn_{q+k}n_{p-k}(1-e^{\epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_p-\epsilon_q})}
	{\beta(\epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_p-\epsilon_q)^2}
\end{align*}
while the exchange term will exhibit the same behavior, both in terms of dependence on $\beta$
and in terms of point-wise convergence. The series $A$ is quite straightforward: we bound the
Fermi-Dirac distributions with their supremum and write
\begin{align*}
	A \leq \sum_k \frac{\hat{V}(k)^2e^{-\delta(\epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_p-\epsilon_q)}}
	{\epsilon_{q+k}+\epsilon_{p-k}-\epsilon_p-\epsilon_q}.
\end{align*}
Let's now discuss $B$. Distributing the product at the numerator, the first term is a
product of Fermi-Dirac distributions divided by $\beta$, which means it is monotonically
decreasing in $\beta$ for all $q,p,k$ outside the Fermi ball (again with the caveat that
$k\not\in\mathcal{M}$). As for the exponential term one can check that the resulting general
term is monotonically decreasing in $\beta$ far enough from the origin, as the only extremal
point is a maximum that moves towards the origin as $k$ grows. Therefore, once again, the
supremum is found at $\beta=\delta$ and the series of the supremum converges, which shows that
the second order is almost entirely uniformly convergent too.
\\
Following the steps taken for the first order, we call $E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}}$ the direct
contribution to the ground state energy and $E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{X}}$ the exchange contribution:
\begin{align*}
	E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}} + E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{X}}
	&= 2\lambda^2\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathbb{Z}^3\\k\in\mathbb{Z}^3\setminus\mathcal{M}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)^2 n_p n_q n_{q+k} n_{p-k}}{\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p}\\
	&\quad+ 2\lambda^2\sum_{\substack{p,q\in\mathbb{Z}^3\\k\in\mathbb{Z}^3\setminus\mathcal{M}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)\hat{V}(p-q-k) n_p n_q n_{q+k} n_{p-k}}{\epsilon_{p-k}+\epsilon_{q+k}-\epsilon_q-\epsilon_p}.
\end{align*}

We are now left with the scaling in the number of particles of the second order, where we must
conclude that the approach we followed so far must, in some capacity, have flaws. Indeed, the
second order does not reproduce the expected $N$-scaling of the mean field regime, which
suggests the expression we wrote does not faithfully represent the second order contributions
to the ground state energy. Consider the direct term in $E^{(2)}$: from our discussion on
Equation \eqref{eq:ground-state-energy} we expect an $N$ dependence of order
$\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/3})$, but our expression leads to
\begin{align*}
	E_{\mathrm{D}}^{(2)}
	&= -2\lambda^2 \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \sum_{\substack{p,q\in B_{\mathrm{F}}:\\q+k,p-k\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)^2}{\hbar^2((p-k)^2+(q+k)^2-q^2-p^2)}\\
	&= N^{-2}N^{2/3} \sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3} \sum_{\substack{p,q\in B_{\mathrm{F}}:\\q+k,p-k\in B_{\mathrm{F}}}}
	\frac{\hat{V}(k)^2}{k^2+k\cdot(q-p)},
\end{align*}
and changing variables like before with ($p\mapsto p'=\hbar p$, same for $q$) we get
\begin{align*}
	E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}}
	&= N^{2/3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3}\hat{V}(k)^2
	\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\substack{p\in\hbar\mathbb{Z}^3:\\|p|\leq C\\|p-k|\leq C}}
	\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\substack{q\in\hbar\mathbb{Z}^3:\\|q|\leq C\\|q+k|\leq C}}
	\frac{1}{k^2+N^{1/3}k\cdot(q-p)}\\
	&\approx N^{1/3}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^3}\hat{V}(k)^2
	\int\limits_{\substack{|p|\leq C\\|p-N^{-1/3}k|\leq C}}\dx^3p
	\int\limits_{\substack{|q|\leq C\\|q+N^{-1/3}k|\leq C}}\dx^3q
	\frac{1}{N^{-1/3}k^2+k\cdot(q-p)}.
\end{align*}
This suggests that $E^{(2)}_{\mathrm{D}}$ will be of order $N^{1/3}$. We must take this
result and direct our future efforts to a careful analysis of the assumptions we made and the
methods we used to compute the second perturbative order. We remind the reader that there are
still stones that have been left unturned, for example: we did not cover in detail the
identification between Grassmannian and operatorial perturbative series, and we avoided the
use of Matsubara frequencies. Most importantly, there are still alternative approaches, chief
among all: the particle-hole transformation, which is what has been used to derive the exact
results in \cite{HPRArt} and \cite{BenArt}.
