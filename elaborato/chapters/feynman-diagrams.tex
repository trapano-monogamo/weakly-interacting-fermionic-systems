\section{Perturbation Theory}

In the previous chapter we built the second quantization formalism to be able to introduce, in
full generality, this key property that is Wick's theorem. Looking at the Gibbs state functional
\begin{align*}
	\langle\cdot\rangle_\beta=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\,\cdot\,e^{-\beta H})}{\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-\beta H})}
\end{align*}
we can imagine expanding the exponential in the numerator as (something similar to) a power
series, and consider each term as a contribution at a specific order. Such terms will contain
powers of the interaction term, i.e. polynomials of creation/annihilation operators. Then
Wick's theorem tells us that at all orders we can write the correct contribution using more
and more complicated combinations of the 2-point function. These combinations can be easily
found by means of the very clever diagrammatic approach first employed by Richard Feynman, and
the aim of this chapter is to outline the inner workings of this approach in the specific case
of a fermionic system at thermal equilibrium.
\begin{remark}
The idea behind the tools we will introduce and the way we will write the perturbative series
is the same as the one used for scattering processes in any QFT. In fact, there is a beautiful
connection between the calculations done for such processes and the theory we build below, and
it has to do with the link between the time evolution operator of quantum mechanics $e^{-iHt}$
and its imaginary time version $e^{-\tau H}$, which defines a Gibbs state.
\end{remark}
The following definitions and theorems are taken, and partially reworked, from lecture notes
on quantum statistical mechanics [\cite{GalNotes}].

\subsection{A toy model: bosonic expectations}
\label{sec:toy-model}

We build the theory of Feynman diagrams by first analyzing at a simple model: a classical
Hamiltonian given by polynomials of complex variables. We will introduce expectations
as Gaussian integrals over such variables and show that they exactly reproduce Wick's theorem,
in a sense that will be made clear below. After that, we will interpret this tool as a formal
way of writing expectations of bosonic (i.e. \textit{commuting}) operators, and finally
introduce an algebraic structure capable of carrying the results to the fermionic (i.e.
\textit{anticommuting}) case.

Suppose we are given a classical system of $N$ coupled harmonic oscillators: a linear chain
model, with Hamiltonian
\begin{align*}
	H_0 = \sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_N} \left( \frac{p_j^2}{2} + (q_{j+1} - q_j)^2 \right).
\end{align*}
It can be shown that there exists a canonical transformation that sends $H_0$ into
\begin{align*}
	H_0 = \sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}} \omega_k z_k^* z_k
\end{align*}
where $\omega_k = 2\sin(\frac{\pi k}{N})$ and $\mathcal{D}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:-\frac{N}{2}\leq k\leq \frac{N+1}{2}\}$.
Following the classical theory, the partition function for this system is
\begin{align*}
	Z_0(\beta) \propto \int \left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k\right) e^{-\beta H_0}
	= \prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}} \int\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k e^{-\beta\omega_k z^*_k z_k}
	\propto \prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}} \frac{1}{\beta\omega_k}
\end{align*}
where, recall, the integral is given by
\begin{align*}
	\int\dx z^*\,\dx z f(z,z^*) \coloneq 2i\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} \dx x\,\dx y f(x+iy,x-iy).
\end{align*}
Suppose now we want to add anharmonic corrections to the model. We start with a quartic term,
and already we notice that exact diagonalization is not possible anymore. Performing the
canonical transformation into the variables $z_k$ and $z_k^*$ leads to
\begin{align*}
	H
	&= H_0 + V = \sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_N}\left( \frac{p_j^2}{2} + (q_{j+1}-q_j)^2 + \frac{\lambda}{4}(q_{j+1}-q_j)^4 \right)\\
	&= \sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\omega_k z^*_k z_k
	+ \lambda\sum_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\Omega_{p,q,k} z^*_{q+k} z^*_{p-k} z_p z_q.
\end{align*}
Now the partition function reads as
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:partition-function-correction}
	Z(\beta,\lambda) = \int\left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k\right)e^{-\beta H_0}e^{-\beta\lambda V},
\end{align}
and this is not exactly computable. If we suppose that $\lambda\ll1$, we can expand the
exponential of the correction term as a power series and truncate $Z(\beta,\lambda)$ to the
desired order. For example
\begin{align*}
	Z(\beta,\lambda)
	&= \int\left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k\right)e^{-\beta H_0}
	\Bigg(1 - \lambda\beta V + \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2)\Bigg)\\
	&= Z_0(\beta)\left(1 - \lambda\beta
		\frac{\int\left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k\right)e^{-\beta H_0}V}{Z_0(\beta)}
		+ \mathcal{O}(\lambda^2)
	\right).
\end{align*}
Looking at this first order correction, we could interpret the expression
\begin{align*}
		\frac{\left(\prod_{k\in\mathcal{D}}\dx z^*_k\,\dx z_k\right)e^{-\beta H_0}}{Z_0(\beta)}
		\eqcolon P_0(\dx z)
\end{align*}
as some Gaussian probability measure, and correspondigly interpret $\int P_0(\dx z) V$ as a
kind of average of $V$. Higher order corrections can also be interpreted as higher moments of
V with respect to this Gaussian probability measure.

We now want to show that this kind of expectation satisfies Wick's theorem, or Isserlis' theorem
as it is known in the field of probability theory. After that it will be clear that the
variables $z^*_k$ and $z_k$ may just as well be regarded to as a formal representation of
creation/annihilation operators, and higher order corrections to the free partition function
$Z_0(\beta)$ are going to be expectations of powers of the interaction potential $V$. The only
obstacle is then the fact that complex Gaussian integrals actually satisfy the \textit{bosonic}
version of Wick's theorem, and this is a consequence of $z^*_k$ and $z_k$ being commuting
variables rather than anticommuting. The missing fermionic signs will be the subject of
Section \ref{sec:grassmann}.

We start our discussion of bosonic expectations by recalling some properties of complex
Gaussian integrals.

\begin{definition}[Complex covariance]
	A matrix $G\in\mathrm{Mat}_{N\times N}$ is said to be a \textit{complex covariance} if
	\begin{itemize}
		\item[(i)] there exists a matrix $U$ that diagonalizes $G$;
		\item[(ii)] all eigenvalues of $G$ have positive real part.
	\end{itemize}
	The space of complex covariances on will be denoted by $\mathcal{C}_N$.
\end{definition}
To ease the notation, if $\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{C}^N$ we will write
$\boldsymbol{z}\cdot \boldsymbol{w}=\sum_{j=1}^N z_j w_j$.

\begin{lemma}
	Let $G\in\mathcal{C}_N$ and $\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{C}^N$, then
	\begin{align*}
		\int\left(\prod_{j=1}^N\frac{\dx z^*_j\,\dx z_j}{2\pi i}\right)
		e^{-\boldsymbol{z}\cdot G^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}} = \det(G).
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Because $G\in\mathcal{C}_N$ there exists $U$ that diagonalizes $G$, i.e. there exist
	$\xi$ such that $\boldsymbol{z}=U\boldsymbol{\xi}$. Then
	\begin{gather*}
		\boldsymbol{z}^* \cdot G^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}
		= \boldsymbol{z}^*\cdot U^\dagger G^{-1}U\boldsymbol{z} \eqcolon \boldsymbol{\xi}^*\cdot D^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\\
		\dx\boldsymbol{z}^*\,\dx\boldsymbol{z} = \det(U^\dagger)\det(U)\dx\boldsymbol{\xi}^*\,\dx\boldsymbol{\xi}
		= \dx\boldsymbol{\xi}^*\,\dx\boldsymbol{\xi}
	\end{gather*}
	where $D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N)$ is the diagonalization of $G$. Then, the left
	hand side becomes
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{l.h.s.}
		= \prod_{j=1}^N\int\frac{\dx \xi^*_j\,\dx \xi_j}{2\pi i} e^{-\xi^*j\frac{1}{\lambda_j}\xi_j}
		= \prod_{j=1}^N\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}\frac{\dx x\,\dx y}{\pi}e^{-\frac{x^2+y^2}{\lambda_j}}
		= \det(G).
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Complex Gaussian measure]
	Let $G\in\mathcal{C}_N$. The complex integration measure
	\begin{align*}
		P_G(\dx z) \coloneq \frac{1}{\det(G)}\int\left(\prod_{j=1}^N\frac{\dx z^*_j\,\dx z_j}{2\pi i}\right)
		e^{-\boldsymbol{z}^*\cdot G^{-1}\boldsymbol{z}}
	\end{align*}
	is called \textit{complex Gaussian measure} of covariance $G$.
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	The matrix $G$ is alsa called a propagator because, as will be made clear by the theorems
	we prove, its matrix elements form exactly the 2-point function for a given system.
\end{remark}

Note that a complex Gaussian measure is ``normalized", in the sense that $\int P_G(\dx z)=1$.
We are now ready to define expectation values and prove their foundamental properties.

\begin{definition}[Complex/bosonic expectation]
	Given $P_G(\dx z)$ and a measureable function $f$, the \textit{expectation of f} with
	respect to $P_G(\dx z)$ is defined as
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G(f) \coloneq \int P_G(\dx z) f(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*).
	\end{align*}
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	In Section \ref{sec:toy-model}, we actually had $G=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{1}{\beta\omega_1},\ldots,\frac{1}{\beta\omega_N}\right)$
	and the first order correction to the partition function was indeed $\mathbb{E}_G(V)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{lemma}
	\label{lemma:source-terms}
	Let $G\in\mathcal{C}_N$ and let $\boldsymbol{J}\in\mathbb{C}^N$ be a fixed complex vector,
	often called a \textit{source term}. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\int P_G(\dx z) e^{\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot\boldsymbol{z} + \boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}^*}
		= e^{\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot G\boldsymbol{J}}
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	Let $U$ be the diagonalization matrix for $G$, let $\boldsymbol{z}=U\boldsymbol{\xi}$ and
	$\boldsymbol{J}=U\boldsymbol{\eta}$. Then we can rewrite the exponent of the integrand
	and complete the square:
	\begin{align*}
		-\boldsymbol{z}^*\cdot G^{-1}\boldsymbol{z} + \boldsymbol{z}^*\cdot\boldsymbol{J} + \boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}^*
		&= -\boldsymbol{\xi}^*\cdot D^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi} + \boldsymbol{\xi}^*\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta} + \boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}^*\\
		&= -(\boldsymbol{\xi}^*-D\boldsymbol{\eta}^*)\cdot D^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-D\boldsymbol{\eta}) + \boldsymbol{\eta}^*\cdot D\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
		&= -(\boldsymbol{\xi}^*-D\boldsymbol{\eta}^*)\cdot D^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-D\boldsymbol{\eta}) + \boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot G\boldsymbol{J}
	\end{align*}
	where, once again, $D=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_N)$ is the diagonalization of
	$G$. We can apply the change of variables $\xi_j\mapsto\xi_j+\frac{1}{\lambda_j}\eta_j$ and
	take the term $J^*\cdot GJ$ out of the integral, but the remaining integral is just $\int
	P_G(\dx\xi)=1$, and the thesis follows immediately.
\end{proof}

To prove the next theorem we will need the two following identities:
\begin{align}
	\label{eq:source-terms}
	P_G(\dx z)z_j = \frac{\partial}{\partial J^*_j}\left(P_G(\dx z)e^{\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}^*}\right)
	\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{J}^*=0}\nonumber\\
	P_G(\dx z)z^*_j = \frac{\partial}{\partial J_j}\left(P_G(\dx z)e^{\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{J}\cdot\boldsymbol{z}^*}\right)
	\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{J}^*=0}
\end{align}

\begin{theorem}[Isserlis' theorem]
	\label{thm:isserlis}
	Given $G\in\mathcal{C}_N$, let $M$ be a positive integer and consider two sets of indices:
	$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_M$ and $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_M$. Then it holds that
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G(z_{\alpha_1}\cdots z_{\alpha_M} z^*_{\beta_1}\cdots z^*_{\beta_M})
		= \sum_{\sigma\in S_N} \prod_{j=1}^M G_{\alpha_j,\beta_{\sigma(j)}}
	\end{align*}
	where $S_M$ is the set of permutations of $M$ elements.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	Using the identities in (\ref{eq:source-terms}) and Lemma (\ref{lemma:source-terms}) we
	can write
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G(z_{\alpha_1}\cdots z_{\alpha_M} z^*_{\beta_1}\cdots z^*_{\beta_M})
		= \frac{\partial^{2M}}{\partial J^*_{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial J^*_{\alpha_M}\partial J_{\beta_1}\cdots\partial J_{\beta_M}}
		\left(e^{\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot G\boldsymbol{J}}\right)\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{J}^*=0}
		= \star
	\end{align*}
	Now we can expand the exponential as a power series and derive term by term. In fact,
	when deriving $2M$ times the $n$-th term of this series we have the following cases: if
	$n<M$ we are taking derivatives of order higher than the degree of the polynomial so it
	gives zero, if $n>M$ deriving will lead to a homogeneous polynomial evaluated at zero so
	it gives zero, if $n=M$ some terms survive. Indeed, if we look at the derivatives with
	respect to the $J^*$ we see that
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial^{M}}{\partial J^*_{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial J^*_{\alpha_M}}
		\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{J}^*\cdot G\boldsymbol{J}\right)^M}{M!}
		&= \frac{1}{M!}\frac{\partial^{M}}{\partial J^*_{\alpha_1}\cdots\partial J^*_{\alpha_M}}
		\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^M J^*_{\rho_j}G_{\rho_j,\tau_k}J_{\tau_k}\right)^M\\
		&= \frac{\partial^{M-1}}{\partial J^*_{\alpha_2}\cdots\partial J^*_{\alpha_M}}
		\frac{M}{M!}\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^M \delta_{\rho_j,\alpha_1}G_{\rho_j,\tau_k}J_{\tau_k}\right)
		\left(\sum_{j,k=1}^M J^*_{\rho_j}G_{\rho_j,\tau_k}J_{\tau_k}\right)^{M-1}\\
		&= \prod_{\ell=1}^M\sum_{j,k=1}^M\delta_{\rho_j,\alpha_\ell}G_{\alpha_\ell,\tau_k}J_{\tau_k}.
	\end{align*}
	Using this in $\star$ we get that
	\begin{align*}
		\star = \frac{\partial^M}{\partial J_{\beta_1}\cdots\partial J_{\beta_M}}
		\left(\prod_{j=1}^M\sum_{k=1}^MG_{\alpha_j,\tau_k}J_{\tau_k}\right)\Bigg|_{J=J^*=0}
	\end{align*}
	and applying Leibnitz rule we arrive at the thesis.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Grassmann integrals and fermionic expectations}
\label{sec:grassmann}

It is time to introduce the anticipated algebraic structure that carries the results found so
far to the fermionic case: Grassmann variables. Consider the unital complex Grassmann algebra
generated by a set of $2N$ anticommuting vectors:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
	\label{def:grassmann}
		&\mathcal{G}_N = \mathrm{span}\{ 1, \psi_1, \bar\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_N, \bar\psi_N \} \\
		&\{ \psi_i, \psi_j \}         = 0 \quad \forall i,j=1,...,N \\
		&\{ \psi_i, \bar\psi_j \}     = 0 \quad \forall i,j=1,...,N \\
		&\{ \bar\psi_i, \bar\psi_j \} = 0 \quad \forall i,j=1,...,N
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\{\psi, \phi\} := \psi\phi + \phi\psi$ is the anticommutator. We need to define
Gaussian integrals and expectations of Grassmann variables.
\\
Given $\mathcal{G}_N$ the Grassmann algebra generated by
$\{\psi_1, ..., \psi_N, \bar\psi_1, ..., \bar\psi_N\}$, denote by
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{G}_N^{-} &= \{
		\phi \in \mathcal{G}_N : [\phi, \psi] = 0 \;\forall \psi \in \mathcal{G}_N
	\} \\
		\mathcal{G}_N^{+} &= \{
		\phi \in \mathcal{G}_N : \{\phi, \psi\} = 0 \;\forall \psi \in \mathcal{G}_N
	\}
\end{align*}
the even (commuting) and odd (anticommuting) subsets of the algebra. It is easy to show that
these are subspaces (by linearity of commutators and anticommutators), and one can also show
that $\mathcal{G}_N$ can be decomposed as
\begin{align*}
	\mathcal{G}_N \cong \mathcal{G}_N^{+} \oplus \mathcal{G}_N^{-}.
\end{align*}
Odd elements satisfy a really important property. If $\psi \in \mathcal{G}_N^{+}$ it follows
that $\{\psi,\psi\} = 2\psi^2 = 0$. That is, all powers greater than one of an odd element vanish.

\begin{definition}[Functions of Grassmann variables]
	Given $\mathcal{G}$ a Grassmann algebra, as defined above, and $f : \mathbb{C}^k \to \mathbb{C}$
	an analytic function given by its Taylor series centered at zero
	\begin{align*}
		f(\boldsymbol{z}) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty \sum_{|\alpha| = j}
		\frac{\partial^\alpha f(0)}{j!} \boldsymbol{z}^i
	\end{align*}
	we define the associated \textit{function of Grassmann variables} as
	\begin{align*}
		f(\boldsymbol{\psi}) = \sum_{j=0}^\infty \sum_{|\alpha| = j}
		\frac{\partial^\alpha f(0)}{j!} \boldsymbol{\psi}^i
	\end{align*}
	where $\boldsymbol{\psi} \in \mathcal{G}_N \times ... \times \mathcal{G}_N$ is the list of
	$k$ Grassmann variables.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	If $\psi \in \mathcal{G}_N^{+}$ it follows that any function of Grassmann variables evaluated
	in $\psi$ has at most first order terms, as all higher orders will always contain a factor
	of $\psi^2 = 0$. The most important example to us is the exponential of an odd element:
	\begin{align*}
		e^{\psi} = 1 + \psi + \frac{1}{2}\psi^2 + ... = 1 + \psi
	\end{align*}
\end{remark}

We now introduce integrals of functions of Grassmann variables. Note that the definition is
going to be purely algebraic and, in particular, motivated by our need to reproduce the
fermionic version of Wick's theorem. We ask, first of all, for the integral to be a linear
operator on a Grassmann algebra. Then, specifying the image of a basis for the algebra will
uniquely determine the operator. The specific definition of the image of basis elements is
such that a Grassmann integral over all such basis elements extracts the coefficient of the
highest degree term of the integrand.
\begin{definition}[Grassmann integral]
	Given $\{1, \psi_1, ..., \psi_N, \bar\psi_1, ..., \bar\psi_N\}$ the anticommuting basis
	for a Grassmann algebra, the \textit{Grassmann integral} is the linear operator that acts
	on this basis as
	\begin{align*}
		&\int\dx\psi_i\,\psi_j = \int\dx\bar\psi_i\,\bar\psi_j \coloneq \delta_{ij}, \\
		&\int\dx\psi_i = \int\dx\bar\psi_i \coloneq 0.
	\end{align*}
	Repeated integration is denoted by a single integral symbol and multiple ``differentials".
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
	In $\mathcal{G}_2$, consider the exponential function. Using the definition, we can
	evaluate the following integrals to see how this operator behaves.
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\int\dx\psi_1\,e^{\psi_1\bar\psi_2} = \int\dx\psi_1\,(1 + \psi_1\bar\psi_2) = \bar\psi_2$;
		\item $\int\dx\bar\psi_2\,e^{\psi_1\bar\psi_2} = \int\dx\bar\psi_2\,(1 - \bar\psi_2\psi_1) = -\psi_1$;
		\item $\int\dx\psi_1\dx\bar\psi_2\,e^{\psi_1\bar\psi_2} = -1$.
	\end{itemize}
\end{example}

\begin{remark}
	Repeated integration, i.e. the composition of multiple integral operators, inherits some form of
	anticommutativity when acting on the basis elements. Indeed in $\mathcal{G}_1$, for example, we have
	\begin{align}
		\label{anticommuting-integrals}
		\int\dx\psi\dx\bar\psi\,\psi\bar\psi
		= -\int\dx\psi\dx\bar\psi\,\bar\psi\psi = -1 = -\int\dx\bar\psi\dx\psi\,\psi\bar\psi.
	\end{align}
	And given the definition of Grassmann functions, this property will be quite useful to us.
\end{remark}

Having defined integration, we can now introduce a \textit{Gaussian measure}, again in a purely
algebraic sense. To make the notation ligher, we define a ``dot product", without any geometric
meaning, as $\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi} \coloneq \sum_{i = 1}^k \psi_i\phi_i$.

\begin{definition}[Grassmann covariance]
	A matrix $G \in \mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})$ is called a \textit{Grassmann covariance} on
	the Grassmann algebra $\mathcal{G}_N$.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}
	Let $G$ be a Grassmann covariance, and let $\boldsymbol{\psi} = (\psi_1, \ldots, \psi_N)$
	and $\boldsymbol{\bar\psi} = (\bar\psi_1, \ldots, \bar\psi_N)$. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\int\left( \prod_{i=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_i \dx\psi_i \right)
		e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi} \cdot G^{-1} \boldsymbol{\psi}} = \mathrm{det}(G^{-1})
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
	We carry out the calculation explicitly.
	\begin{align*}
		\int\left( \prod_{i=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_i \dx\psi_i \right)
		e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi} \cdot G^{-1} \boldsymbol{\psi}}
		&= \int\left( \prod_{i=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_i \dx\psi_i \right)
		\frac{(-1)^N}{N!}\left( \sum_{i,j=1}^N \bar\psi_i (G^{-1})_{ij} \psi_j \right)^N \\
		&= \int\left( \prod_{i=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_i \dx\psi_i \right)
		\frac{1}{N!}\left( \sum_{i,j=1}^N \bar\psi_i (G^{-1})_{ij} \psi_j \right)^N \\
		&= \int\left( \prod_{i=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_i \dx\psi_i \right)
		\frac{1}{N!} \sum_{\sigma,\tau \in \mathrm{S}_N} \prod_{j=1}^N (G^{-1})_{\sigma(j),\tau(j)} \bar\psi_{\sigma(j)} \psi_{\tau(j)}
		= \star
	\end{align*}
	Where $\mathrm{S}_N$ is the set of permutations of $N$ elements. We can split the product
	into a product of matrix elements times a product of Grassmann variables, then we use that
	\begin{align*}
		\prod_{j=1}^N \bar\psi_{\sigma(j)}\psi_{\tau(j)} = (-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) + \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)} \prod_{j=1}^N \bar\psi_j \psi_j
	\end{align*}
	to find that
	\begin{align*}
		\star &= \frac{1}{N!} \sum_{\sigma,\tau \in \mathrm{S}_N} (-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma) + \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)}
		\prod_{j=1}^N (G^{-1})_{\sigma(j),\tau(j)} \\
		&= \frac{1}{N!} \sum_{\sigma \in \mathrm{S}_N} (-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)}
		\prod_{j=1}^N (G^{-1})_{j,\sigma{j}} \\
		&= \mathrm{det}(G^{-1})
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Grassmann Gaussian measure]
	Let $G$ be a Gaussian covariance. A \textit{Grassmann Gaussian measure} is the Grassmann
	integral measure defined by
	\begin{align*}
		P_G(\dx\psi) \coloneq \frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(G^{-1})}
		\left( \prod_{j=1}^N \dx\bar\psi_j \dx\psi_j \right)
		e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi} \cdot G^{-1} \boldsymbol{\psi}}
	\end{align*}
	where $\psi_i$ and $\bar\psi_i$ are the usual anticommuting Grassmann variables.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
	The factor in front of the differentials guarantees a ``normalized" measure, i.e.\\
	$\int P_G(\dx\psi) = (1/\mathrm{det}(G^{-1})) \mathrm{det}(G^{-1}) = 1$.
\end{remark}

We will prove the following statement later.

\begin{lemma}
	With the same notation used so far,
	\begin{align*}
		\int P_G(\dx\psi) \psi_i \bar\psi_j = G_{ij}
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

The following are useful facts about Grassmann integrals that we give without proof, we just
point out that proving these only involves taking good care of the combinatorics that come
from the definition of Grassmann integrals, and nothing more.

\begin{lemma}
	Let $D \in \mathrm{GL(2,\mathbb{C})}$, $\boldsymbol{\varphi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\eta}$
	two vectors of Grassmann variables. Then, under the following change of variables
	\begin{align*}
		\begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\psi} \\ \boldsymbol{\bar\psi} \end{pmatrix}
		= D \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\varphi} \\ \boldsymbol{\bar\varphi} \end{pmatrix}
		+ \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{\eta} \\ \boldsymbol{\bar\eta} \end{pmatrix}
	\end{align*}
	the integral of a function of Grassmann variables $f$ changes as follows
	\begin{align*}
		\int \left( \prod_{i} \dx\bar\psi_i \psi_i \right) f(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\bar\psi})
	= \mathrm{det}(G^{-1}) \int \left( \prod_{i} \dx\bar\varphi_i \varphi_i \right) f(D(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\bar\varphi})+(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\bar\eta}))
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
	Let $G \in \mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})$, then
	\begin{align*}
		\int P_G(\dx\psi)\,e^{\boldsymbol{\bar\eta \cdot \psi} + \boldsymbol{\bar\psi \cdot \eta}}
			= e^{\boldsymbol{\bar\eta \cdot} G \boldsymbol{\eta}}
	\end{align*}
\end{lemma}

Before defining expectations, we need one more operator on a Grassmann algebra to mimic the
behaviour of these same integrals done in the complex variables case: derivatives of Grassmann
functions. If we are given a polynomial of degree $N$ in $N$ variables, then deriving this
polynomial in all of the variables should return the maximal degree term. We will take this as
the defining property of derivation, as we defined functions to be nothing more than Taylor
series. Now notice that integrating a Grassmann function in all variables (i.e., in all the
basis elements) returns exactly the same thing, the coefficient of the maximal degree term.

\begin{definition}[Grassmann derivatives]
	Let $f(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\bar\psi})$ be a Grassmann function, the
	\textit{Grassmann derivatives} of $f$ with respect to the basis elements are the linear
	operators defined as
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi_i}f \coloneq \int\dx\psi_i\,f(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\bar\psi})
	\end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}[Proof (Lemma 2.2)]
	Recalling the ``anticommutativity property" of Grassmann integrals from Equation (\ref{anticommuting-integrals}),
	it is clear that the same property holds for derivatives too. We can make use of Grassmann
	derivatives to prove this lemma in a way that is closer to the complex variables case.
	Given a vector of Grassmann variables $\boldsymbol{\eta}$, we can write something similar
	to (\ref{eq:source-terms}):
\begin{align*}
	\psi_i\bar\psi_j e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi}\cdot G^{-1} \boldsymbol{\psi}}
		= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\eta_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_j}
		e^{-\boldsymbol{\bar\psi}\cdot G^{-1} \boldsymbol{\psi}
		+ \boldsymbol{\bar\eta} \cdot \boldsymbol{\psi} + \boldsymbol{\bar\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}}
		\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\bar\eta} = 0}.
\end{align*}
We can substitute this in the integral to get that
\begin{align*}
	\int P_G(\dx\psi)\, \psi_i\bar\psi_j
	&= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\eta_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_j} \int P_G(\dx\psi)
	e^{\boldsymbol{\bar\eta} \cdot \boldsymbol{\psi} + \boldsymbol{\bar\psi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}}
	\bigg|_0 \\
	&= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\eta_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_j}
	e^{\boldsymbol{\bar\eta} \cdot G \boldsymbol{\eta}} \bigg|_0 \\
	&= -\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar\eta_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_j}
	(\bar\eta_i G_{ij} \eta_j) \bigg|_0 \\
	&= G_{ij}
\end{align*}
which is exactly the thesis.
\end{proof}

We are finally ready to prove that Gaussian Grassmann integrals satisfy Wick's theorem.

\begin{theorem}[Wick's theorem]
	Let $M \in \mathbb{N}$ be the degree of a monomial in Grassmann variables, and let
	$A = \{a_1, \ldots, a_M\}$ and $B = \{b_1, \ldots, b_M\}$ be two sets of M indices. The
	integral of a monomial is
	\begin{align*}
		\int P_G(\dx\psi) \left( \prod_{\alpha\in A,\beta\in B} \psi_{\alpha}\bar\psi_{\beta} \right)
		= \sum_{\sigma \in \mathrm{S}_N} (-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)} \prod_{j=1}^M G_{a_j,b_{\sigma(j)}}
	\end{align*}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	The proof is almost identical to the one for Theorem (\ref{thm:isserlis}), with the only
	difference being that now we have to be careful with the sign of the permutations. In
	particular, deriving the $M$-th term of the exponential carries an additional factor of
	$(-1)^M$:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathrm{l.h.s.} = (-1)^M\left(\prod_{\alpha\in A,\beta\in B}
		\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{J}_\alpha} \frac{\partial}{\partial J_\beta}\right)
		e^{\boldsymbol{\bar J}\cdot G\boldsymbol{J}} \bigg|_{\boldsymbol{J}=\boldsymbol{\bar{J}}=0}.
	\end{align*}
	The rest of the proof follows exactly as in Theorem (\ref{thm:isserlis}).
\end{proof}

At this point, we can take this notion of Gaussian integrals to write the fermionic version
of Section \ref{sec:toy-model}.

\subsection{Perturbative series and Feynman diagrams}

In this section we are finally going to look at how the order-by-order corrections introduced
by a term like $e^{-\beta\lambda V}$ in Equation (\ref{eq:partition-function-correction}) can,
in fact, be written in terms of Feynman diagrams, which are nothing more than a graphical
way to use Wick's theorem and produce valid contractions in n-point functions. To do so, we
introduce the concept of a truncated expectation. This is the object that ultimately will
contain the important diagrams. Both bosonic and fermionic cases follow the same procedure but
present some slight differences here and there, which we shall point out when needed.

Suppose we have an interaction potential $V$, formally written as a homogeneous polynomial of
complex or Grassmann variables. We can represent such a potential as a \textit{vertex}, i.e. a
node in a graph, with as many labelled outgoing lines as there are conjugate variables in the
expression of $V$, and as many labelled incoming lines as there are plain variables. For
example, our quartic correction of Section \ref{sec:toy-model} would be the formal way of
writing a two-body interaction potential $V=\sum_{p,q,k}\hat{V}(k)a^*_{q+k}a^*_{p-k}a_pa_q$
and its vertex would be
\begin{align*}
	V = 
	\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8, baseline=(current bounding box.center)]
		\node (a) at (-1, 1) {} node[scale=0.7] at (-1.1, 0.5) {$p-k$};
		\node (b) at ( 1, 1) {} node[scale=0.7] at ( 1.1, 0.5) {$q+k$};
		\node (c) at (-1,-1) {} node[scale=0.7] at (-0.9,-0.5) {$q$};
		\node (d) at ( 1,-1) {} node[scale=0.7] at ( 0.9,-0.5) {$p$};
		\begin{scope}[very thick, decoration={
				markings,
				mark=at position 0.57 with {\arrow{>}}}
			]
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (0,0) -- (a);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (0,0) -- (b);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (c) -- (0,0);
			\draw[black, thick, postaction={decorate}] (d) -- (0,0);
		\end{scope}
		\filldraw[black] (0,0) circle (2pt);
	\end{tikzpicture}.
\end{align*}
The \textit{value} of a vertex is defined exactly as the specific expression of $V$. In a
diagram, vertices can be connected by lines called \textit{propagators} or even
\textit{contractions}, and as the name suggests the value of such propagators is given by the
2-point function. In particular, if a line labelled $i$ exits a vertex and enters another
as a line labelled $j$, then to such a line we associate the value $G_{ij}$. A vertex may
have an outgoing leg connecting back to an incoming line in the same vertex, and in this work
we will call this \textit{a loop}. The \textit{value of a diagram $\Gamma$}, denoted
$\mathrm{val}(\Gamma)$, is obtained by multiplying the values of vertices and propagators
following the labelling and contractions dictated by the diagram.
\\
The definitions of the vertices and the propagators involved in the diagrams are called
\textit{Feynman rules}. Finally, even though we will not make use of such a definition in our
work, a Feynman rule may contain many different ``types" of incoming/outgoing lines and
associated propagators. This means that in the vertex there might be both fermionic and bosonic
variables, and some of them may belong to different sets of variables (for example different
Grassmann algebras to represent different families of fermions). Each type of line gets its
own propagator, and they should be distinguished graphically.
\\
We will compute values of Feynman diagrams explicitly in the next chapter, specifically in
Section \ref{second-order}, so we refer to those computations as examples of
the general rules we just laid out.

We use diagrams to compute expectations. If $V$ is our interaction potential, its expectation
$\mathbb{E}_G(V)$ is the sum of all possible Wick contractions, as we proved, i.e. all
possible diagrams with one vertex and all lines contracted. Adding more copies of $V$ inside
the expectation means adding vertices, while multiplying by other expectations means
multiplying the values of the respective sums of diagrams. We will prove that the most
important diagrams are actually the connected ones. 

\begin{definition}[Truncated expectation]
	In the bosonic case, let $P_G(\dx z)$ be a complex Gaussian measure, and let
	$V_1,\ldots,V_s$ be $s$ polynomials complex variables. We define the \textit{truncated
	expectation} of $V_1,\ldots,V_s$ as
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_s) \coloneq
		\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\partial^s}{\partial t_1\cdots\partial t_s}\log\left(
			\int P_G(\dx z) e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_j V_j(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*)}\chi(|z|<R)
		\right)\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{t}=0}.
	\end{align*}
	In the fermionic case, let $P_G(\dx\psi)$ be a Grassmann Gaussian measure, and let
	$V_1,\ldots,V_s$ be $s$ polynomials of Grassmann variables. We define the \textit{truncated
	expectation} of $V_1,\ldots,V_s$ as
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_s)\coloneq
		\frac{\partial^s}{\partial t_1\cdots\partial t_s}\log\left(
			\int P_G(\dx\psi) e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_j V_j(\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{\bar\psi})}
		\right)\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{t}=0}.
	\end{align*}
	We denote $\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;r)$ the truncated expectation of $r$ copies of the same polynomial
	$V$. Given a multiindex $I=(I_1,\ldots,I_n)$, we will denote $\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_I)
	=\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{I_1},\ldots,V_{I_n})$
\end{definition}
\begin{remark}
	Note that the special case where $s=1$ reads as $\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;1)=\mathbb{E}_G(V)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[Invariance of exponentials]
	Let $V$ be a polynomial of complex (or Grassmann) variables, and $P_G(\dx z)$ (or $P_G(\dx\psi)$)
	be a complex (or Grassmann) Gaussian measure. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\int P_G(\dx z) e^{V(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*)}
		= e^{\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{1}{j!}\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;j)}.
	\end{align*}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
	We will compute the logarithm of the left hand side in the complex case, and the thesis
	will follow immediately after. The Grassmann case is completely analogous and doesn't
	require any modification. To compute the logarithm we take a Taylor expansion around zero,
	and note that in the Grassmann case this just coincides with the definition of the integrand.
	\begin{align*}
		\log\int P_G(\dx z) e^{V(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*)}
		&= \log\int P_G(\dx z)e^{tV(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*)}\big|_{t=1}\\
		&= \sum_{j=0}^\infty \frac{1}{j!}\frac{\dx^j}{\dx s^j} \log\left(
		\log\int P_G(\dx z)e^{tV(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^*)}
		\right)^j \bigg|_{s=0} t^j \bigg|_{t=1} = \star.
	\end{align*}
	But now we notice that this contains exactly the definition of $\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;j)$,
	thus:
	\begin{align*}
		\star = \sum_{j=0}^\infty \frac{1}{j!}\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;j)t^j \big|_{t=1}
		= \sum_{j=0}^\infty \frac{1}{j!}\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;j)
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Linked cluster theorem]
	\label{thm:linked-cluster}
	Let $V_1,\ldots,V_s$ be polynomials of complex (or Grassmann) variables. Then
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_s) = \mathbb{E}_G(V_1V_2\cdots V_s)
		- \sum_{p=2}^s \sum_{\{I_1,\ldots,I_p\}\in P_p} \prod_{j=1}^p \mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{I_j})
	\end{align*}
	where $P_p$ is the set of all possible partitions of the set of indices $\{1,\ldots,s\}$
	into $p$ disjoint subsets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	We consider the complex case to outline the proof, but the Grassmann case is even simpler,
	as the characteristic functions that appear below are not needed (we put them in the
	complex case to manage convergence of the integrals). Consider $r\leq s$ indices $i_1,
	\ldots,i_r$, and define the following quantity for $t,R\in\mathbb{R}$:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_1}\cdots V_{i_r})
		&\coloneq \frac{\mathbb{E}_G(V_{i_1}\cdots V_{i_r}e^{\sum_{j=1}^st_jV_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R))}
		{\mathbb{E}_G(e^{\sum_{j=1}^st_jV_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R))}\\
		&= \frac{\int P_G(\dx z)\left(\prod_{j=1}^rV_{i_j}\right)e^{\sum_{j=1}^st_jV_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)}
		{\int P_G(\dx z)e^{\sum_{j=1}^st_jV_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)}
	\end{align*}
	And its truncated version
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{i_1}\cdots V_{i_r})
		= \frac{\partial^r}{\partial t_1\cdots\partial t_r}\left(
			\log\int P_G(\dx z) e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_j V_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)
		\right).
	\end{align*}
	Then $\mathbb{E}_G = \mathbb{E}_{G,0,\infty}$ and $\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}=
	\mathbb{E}_{G,0,\infty}^{\mathrm{T}}$. The aim is to prove the more general formula in
	which we use $\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}$ instead of the regular expectation, and retrieve the
	thesis by setting $t=0$ and taking $R\to\infty$. We will proceed by induction, starting
	from the base case $r=1$:
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}^{\mathrm{T}}(V_k)
		&= \frac{\partial}{\partial t_j}
		\left(\log\int P_G(\dx z)e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_j V_j(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)\right)\\
		&= \frac{\int P_G(\dx z)V_j(z,z^*)e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_s V_s(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)}
		{\int P_G(\dx z)e^{\sum_{j=1}^s t_s V_s(z,z^*)}\chi(|z|<R)}\\
		&= \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_k).
	\end{align*}
	We now assume the modified version of the thesis holds for $r-1$ and prove that the case
	$r$ follows from this. The inductive hypothesis reads
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_{r-1}) = \mathbb{E}_G(V_1V_2\cdots V_{r-1})
		- \sum_{p=2}^{r-1} \sum_{\{I_1,\ldots,I_p\}\in P_p} \prod_{j=1}^p \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{I_j}).
	\end{align*}
	Deriving both sides of the equation by $t_{i_r}$ gives us, by definition,
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i_r}}(\mathrm{l.h.s.})
		= \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{i_1};\ldots;V_{i_r})
	\end{align*}
	and
	\begin{align*}
		\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{i_r}} \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_1},\ldots,V_{i_{r-1}})
		= (\ldots) = \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_1},\ldots,V_{i_r})
		- \mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_1},\ldots,V_{i_{r-1}})\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_r}).
	\end{align*}
	Then, using the inductive hypothesis, we can rewrite the factor $\mathbb{E}_{G,t,R}(V_{i_1},
	\ldots,V_{i_{r-1}})$ and iteratively make all possible partitions in which $i_r$ is left
	alone in the expression, concluding the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}[Connected Feynman diagrams]
	A Feynman diagram is said to be \textit{connected} if it is a connected graph.
\end{definition}

In other words, in a connected Feynman diagrams, every vertex must be accessible to every other
by following propagator lines.

\begin{theorem}[Connected Feynman diagrams and trunacted expectations]
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_s) = \sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{s,\mathrm{C}}}
		\mathrm{val}(\Gamma)
	\end{align*}
	where $\mathcal{G}_{s,\mathrm{C}}$ is the set of all connected diagrams with $s$ vertices.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
	We proceed by induction, with the base case $s=1$ being trivially true, by construction of
	$\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V;1)$. We will call $\mathcal{G}_{s,p,\mathrm{CC}}$ the set of
	all connected diagrams with $s$ vertices divided into $p$ connected components. Assuming
	now the thesis holds for the case $s-1$, we can use the linked cluster theorem to see that
	\begin{align*}
		\mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_1;\ldots;V_s)
		&= \mathbb{E}_G(V_1;\ldots;V_s) - \sum_{p=2}^s \sum_{\{I_1,\ldots,I_p\}\in P_p}
		\prod_{j=1}^p \mathbb{E}_G^{\mathrm{T}}(V_{I_j})\\
		&= \mathbb{E}_G(V_1;\ldots;V_s) - \sum_{p=2}^s \sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{s,p,\mathrm{CC}}}\mathrm{val}(\Gamma)\\
		&= \sum_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{G}_{s,1,\mathrm{CC}}}\mathrm{val}(\Gamma).
	\end{align*}
\end{proof}

In the next chapter we will discuss how we can rewrite the expectations on Gibbs states as a
series of expectations of the powers of the interaction potential, and we will see how the
linked cluster theorem tells us those expectations are actually \textit{truncated}, with a
number of copies of $V$ given by the order at which we decide to truncate the expression.
Ultimately what this means is that our computations will involve, order by order, a sum of all
connected diagrams with a number of vertices equal to the chosen order, as is expected in
diagrammatic perturbation theory.
