\chapter*{Introduction}

Understanding the behavior of quantum systems composed of a large number of particles
is one of the most challenging problems in modern physics. The complexiy of a system generally
grows very rapidly the more degrees of freedom the wavefunction depends on, so much so that
a direct analytical or numerical treatment soon becomes impractical. As a consequence, even
basic questions about the energy, correlations or structure of the ground state may be out of
reach without suitable approximations.
Even without considernig interactions between particles, the difference in bosonic and
fermionic statistics are remarkable and are approached differently. For instance, at very low
temperatures, bosons may undergo a phase transition that leads to the Bose-Einstein
condensate, while fermions do not. Already at this level we see that,
even if the microscopic laws are similar, the macroscopic features of the system may differ.
When interactions are included, the problem becomes even more complex, and one has to
understand the combined effects of statistics, correlations, and collective behavior.

In many cases, a natural way to account for these mechanisms is to identify regimes in which
the full, interacting theory can be replaced -- at least approximately -- by a simpler,
effective theory. The idea is that not all microscopic features of a system contribute equally
to the macroscopic behavior, and one may look for suitable asymptotic regimes in which
the collective effect of many particles can be described by effective quantities that can
be treated directly.

An example of such regimes, and the one which we will explore in this thesis, is that of the
\textit{mean field}. In this regime, the number of particles in the system is
very large, and the interaction between them is rescaled such that the total effect on the
energy of a single particle remains of order one. As a consequence, even though the strength
of the interaction becomes weak, the cumulative effect on each particle is still significant.
One may think of each particle as moving, to leading order, through a field that avareges the
contributions of every other particle. In fermionic systems, the mean field limit combines
two different effects: the averaging of the interaction produced by the scaling in the number
of particles, and the strong constraints imposed by the Pauli exclusion principle.

Even in the mean field limit, however, the physical properties of a system may still be
hard to understand, and this motivates the perturbative approach. One starts from a
sufficiently well understood problem, such as a non-interacting or mean field regime, and
introduces corrections to study the effects of the interactions on the true interacting
ground state. Perturbation theory then allows to gradually introduce and identify finer and
finer corrections to physical quantities, and offers a way to go beyond the leading-order
approximation.

Nevertheless, perturbative arguments are often formal unless they are accompanied by precise
estimates that validate the approximation. Indeed, small errors may accumulate and
perturbative expansions may even fail to converge, and the validity of an approximation may
depend in subtle ways on the scaling of the interaction. For this reason, a mathematically
rigorous approach is not only a matter of formal precision, but rather an essential tool
to determine when the perturbative description is reliable and in what sense. With well
defined and controlled perturbative expansions of physical quantities, one can then make
precise statements about the validity of an approximation and quantify the error between the
effective model and the original problem.

In the present thesis we take a perturbative approach to the study of the ground state
energy of an interacting Fermi gas in the mean field limit. To carry out the computation we
introduce and discuss the main features of second quantization, a convenient formalism in
which to set up perturbation theory. The perturbative parameter is given by the strength of
the two-particle interaction, which is related -- in the mean field limit -- to an inverse
power of the number of particles. We then obtain the scaling of the first order corrections
to the non-interacting ground state energy in agreement with known results obtained via
exact methods. We also begin a preliminary investigation of the second order, but its analysis
remains incomplete because of the difficulties in controlling divergent quantities. We isolate
the problematic terms and discuss the next steps we intend to take in the future to complete
their analysis.
